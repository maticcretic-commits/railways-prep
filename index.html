    Class 10 core

    General science essentials

    Group D science usually tests school-level Physics, Chemistry and Life Science. Learn definitions with units, causes and one application.

    Physics

    Motion & force

    Speed is scalar; velocity has direction. Acceleration is change in velocity per unit time. Newton’s second law: F = ma. Momentum p = mv; impulse equals change in momentum.

    Work = force × displacement · Power = work/time · KE = ½mv²
    Physics

    Electricity

    Current is charge flow per second. Potential difference supplies energy per unit charge. In series, resistance adds; in parallel, reciprocals add.

    V = IR · Power = VI = I²R = V²/R · Energy = Pt
    Physics

    Light & sound

    Reflection obeys angle i = angle r. Convex lenses converge; concave lenses diverge. Sound needs a medium; pitch depends on frequency, loudness on amplitude.

    Wave speed = frequency × wavelength
    Chemistry

    Atoms, elements & compounds

    Atomic number = protons. Mass number = protons + neutrons. Isotopes share atomic number but differ in mass. Ionic bonds transfer electrons; covalent bonds share them.

    Chemistry

    Acids, bases & salts

    Acids give H⁺ in water; bases give OH⁻. pH below 7 is acidic, 7 neutral, above 7 basic. Acid + base → salt + water.

    pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]
    Chemistry

    Metals & reactions

    Oxidation is loss of electrons; reduction is gain. More reactive metals displace less reactive metals from salts. Rusting needs oxygen and moisture.

    Biology

    Cell & tissues

    Cell membrane controls exchange; nucleus holds genetic material; mitochondria release usable energy; chloroplasts perform photosynthesis. Tissues are groups of similar cells with a function.

    Biology

    Human systems

    Alveoli exchange gases. Small intestine absorbs nutrients. Nephrons filter blood. Arteries carry blood away from the heart; veins return it. Insulin lowers blood glucose.

    Biology

    Heredity & ecology

    Genes are units of heredity on chromosomes. Dominant traits appear in heterozygotes. Energy flows one way through food chains; only a fraction passes to the next trophic level.

    Memory drill: write SI unit → formula → common instrument for each quantity: force–newton–spring balance; current–ampere–ammeter; potential difference–volt–voltmeter; power–watt–wattmeter; temperature–kelvin–thermometer.

    History timeline

    1853

    India’s first passenger train ran from Bombay (Bori Bunder) to Thane on 16 April.

    1925

    India’s first electric passenger train ran between Bombay VT and Kurla.

    1951

    Major railway systems were grouped into zones as the network was reorganised.

    1986

    Computerised passenger reservation began in New Delhi.

    Track & operations vocabulary

    Gauge: distance between inner faces of rails; broad gauge in India is 1,676 mm.

    Block section: track portion between two block stations where train movement is controlled.

    Points & crossings: track components that guide vehicles from one track to another.

    Interlocking: arrangement preventing conflicting routes or unsafe signal operation.

    Loco pilot: operates the locomotive/train; guard or train manager oversees train operations from the rear/end position according to duty.

    Signals

    Red: stop. Yellow: caution, be prepared for a restrictive indication. Green: proceed subject to rules. Never reduce real operating rules to colour alone.

    Traction

    Electric traction draws power through overhead equipment and pantograph. Diesel-electric locomotives use a diesel engine to generate electricity for traction motors.

    Safety

    Train protection combines signalling, interlocking, communication, braking discipline and human procedures. Kavach is India’s indigenous automatic train protection system.

    1. Arithmetic

    Arithmetic carries roughly a third of the RRB NTPC maths paper and nearly half of Group D's. The trap is not difficulty — it is speed. Every formula below should be recallable in under three seconds; the shortcuts convert 60-second sums into 20-second sums.

    Number system and divisibility

    Master the divisibility rules: by 3 (digit sum divisible by 3), 4 (last two digits divisible by 4), 6 (divisible by both 2 and 3), 8 (last three digits divisible by 8), 9 (digit sum divisible by 9), 11 (difference of alternate-digit sums is 0 or a multiple of 11). RRB loves asking which of four numbers is divisible by 11 or 9 — the rules decide it in seconds.

    Prime facts: 1 is neither prime nor composite; 2 is the only even prime; the smallest 2-digit prime is 11 and the largest is 97. The number of primes between 1 and 100 is 25 — a recurring one-liner in General Awareness, not just maths.

    LCM × HCF = product of the two numbers. If a question gives one number, the LCM and the HCF, the other number is (LCM × HCF) ÷ given number. Shortcut for the largest number dividing x, y, z leaving remainders r₁, r₂, r₃: compute the HCF of (x−r₁), (y−r₂), (z−r₃). Shortcut for the smallest number which when divided by x, y, z leaves the same remainder r: LCM(x, y, z) + r.

    Percentages — the master key

    Learn fractional equivalents by heart: 12.5% = 1/8, 16.67% = 1/6, 20% = 1/5, 25% = 1/4, 33.33% = 1/3, 37.5% = 3/8, 40% = 2/5, 62.5% = 5/8, 66.67% = 2/3, 75% = 3/4, 83.33% = 5/6, 87.5% = 7/8. Most RRB percentage questions resolve instantly through these.

    Successive changes: a rise of x% followed by a fall of x% is always a net loss of (x²/100)%. Two successive discounts of a% and b% equal a single discount of (a + b − ab/100)%. If a value increases by 20%, the percentage decrease needed to return to the original is 20/120 × 100 = 16.67%.

    Profit, loss and interest

    Profit% and loss% are always calculated on cost price, never on selling price — the most common trap. CP = SP × 100/(100 + P%) for a gain, SP × 100/(100 − L%) for a loss.

    Simple interest: . Compound interest: A = P(1 + r/100)ⁿ. The CI–SI difference for 2 years = P(r/100)² and for 3 years = P(r/100)² × (3 + r/100) — this difference question appears almost every year. When the rate is given per annum but compounded half-yearly, halve the rate and double the time. Amount on successive half-years grows; never apply the annual rate to the whole period.

    Ratio, proportion and averages

    If a : b = 2 : 3 and b : c = 4 : 5, make the common term equal: multiply the first ratio by 4 and the second by 3 → a : b : c = 8 : 12 : 15. Fourth proportional of a, b, c is bc/a; mean proportional of a and b is √(ab).

    Average = sum/n. If the average of n numbers is wrongly recorded as m instead of k, correct the total by adding n(k − m) — the "wrong average" template. Speed shortcut: average speed for equal distances at speeds v₁ and v₂ is 2v₁v₂/(v₁ + v₂), not (v₁ + v₂)/2.

    Time, speed and distance

    Distance = speed × time. Convert km/h to m/s by multiplying by 5/18. A train of length L crossing a pole takes L/speed seconds; crossing a platform of length P takes (L + P)/speed seconds — forgetting to add the platform length is the standard trap.

    Relative speed: same direction → subtract; opposite direction → add. Upstream speed = boat speed − stream speed; downstream = boat speed + stream. If a man rows d km downstream in t₁ hours and returns in t₂ hours, boat speed = d(t₁ + t₂)/(2t₁t₂) and stream speed = d(t₂ − t₁)/(2t₁t₂).

    Time and work

    If A does a job in x days and B in y days, together they take xy/(x + y) days. For three workers, the combined time is xyz/(xy + yz + zx). A pipe filling in x hours and emptying in y hours fills the tank in xy/(y − x) hours when both run. Wages divide in the ratio of work done, i.e. the ratio of 1/x : 1/y — wages follow efficiency, not time taken.

    Exam temperament: in RRB papers, arithmetic questions reward template recognition. The moment you spot the template (successive discount, wrong average, train-platform, CI–SI difference), apply the formula directly instead of deriving it.

    2. Algebra, Geometry & Mensuration

    NTPC's advanced maths and the JE/ALP technical papers test these three areas together. The questions are formula-driven: roughly 80% can be solved by recognising which identity, theorem or area formula applies.

    Algebraic identities

    The big five: (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b²; (a − b)² = a² − 2ab + b²; a² − b² = (a + b)(a − b); (a + b)³ = a³ + 3a²b + 3ab² + b³; a³ − b³ = (a − b)(a² + ab + b²) and a³ + b³ = (a + b)(a² − ab + b²).

    Two workhorses for RRB: if x + 1/x = k, then x² + 1/x² = k² − 2 and x³ + 1/x³ = k³ − 3k. And if a + b + c = 0, then a³ + b³ + c³ = 3abc — a question template that appears in nearly every ALP paper. Also: (a + b + c)² = a² + b² + c² + 2(ab + bc + ca).

    Linear equations in two variables have a unique solution when a₁/a₂ ≠ b₁/b₂, no solution when a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ ≠ c₁/c₂ (parallel lines), and infinitely many when all three ratios are equal (coincident lines). The discriminant D = b² − 4ac decides a quadratic's roots: D > 0 two real roots, D = 0 one repeated root, D < 0 no real roots. Sum of roots = −b/a, product = c/a.

    Triangles and circles

    Pythagoras: a² + b² = c² for a right triangle, with common triplets (3, 4, 5), (5, 12, 13), (8, 15, 17), (7, 24, 25), (20, 21, 29) — memorise these; RRB drops them into "find the third side" questions constantly. The median to the hypotenuse equals half the hypotenuse.

    Area via Heron: s = (a + b + c)/2, area = √[s(s − a)(s − b)(s − c)]. For an equilateral triangle of side a: area = (√3/4)a², height = (√3/2)a, circumradius = a/√3, inradius = a/(2√3) — circumradius is exactly double the inradius.

    Circle: a tangent is perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact; two tangents drawn from an external point are equal in length. Angle in a semicircle is always 90°. Angles in the same segment are equal. The angle subtended by an arc at the centre is double the angle at the circumference. If two chords intersect inside a circle, (segment₁ × segment₂) of one chord equals that of the other.

    Polygon shortcut: an n-sided polygon has n(n − 3)/2 diagonals. Sum of interior angles = (n − 2) × 180°. Each interior angle of a regular polygon = (n − 2) × 180°/n. A quadrilateral's angles always sum to 360°.

    Mensuration — areas

    Rectangle: lb; square: a²; parallelogram: base × height; rhombus: (d₁ × d₂)/2; trapezium: ½ × (sum of parallel sides) × height. Circle: πr², circumference 2πr; semicircle area πr²/2. Sector of angle θ: (θ/360) × πr² with arc length (θ/360) × 2πr. Ring (annulus): π(R² − r²).

    Path problems: area of a path of width w around a rectangle of l × b = 2w(l + b + 2w); around a circle of radius r = πw(2r + w). If each side of a square increases by x%, its area increases by (2x + x²/100)%.

    Mensuration — volumes and surface areas

    Cube: a³, total surface 6a². Cuboid: lbh, surface 2(lb + bh + hl), space diagonal √(l² + b² + h²). Cylinder: πr²h, curved surface 2πrh, total 2πr(h + r). Cone: (1/3)πr²h, slant height l = √(r² + h²), curved surface πrl, total πr(l + r). Sphere: (4/3)πr³, surface 4πr²; hemisphere: (2/3)πr³, total surface 3πr² (the flat face counts — a classic trap where students write 2πr²).

    Scaling rule: if all linear dimensions scale by factor k, areas scale by k² and volumes by k³. If the radius of a sphere doubles, its volume becomes 8 times — RRB asks this in the "how many times" form. Melting one solid into another: equate volumes (volume is conserved, surface area is not).

    Traps to log in your notebook: diameter vs radius mix-ups in circle questions; using slant height where vertical height is needed in cones; forgetting the "+2w" correction in path areas; treating the hemisphere's total surface as 2πr². Each of these has cost candidates a mark in recent papers.

    3. Data Interpretation & Trigonometry

    DI is the highest-marks-per-minute section in NTPC maths if you calculate smart, and the slowest if you calculate everything. Trigonometry is small but near-guaranteed: 3–5 questions built on standard angles and one or two identities.

    Reading DI sets fast

    RRB uses four formats: tables, bar graphs, pie charts, and line graphs. Spend the first 30 seconds noting only three things — the units (₹ crore? thousands? lakh?), the years or categories covered, and whether the question asks for a share, a change, or a comparison. Most errors come from misreading units, not from bad arithmetic.

    Percentage change = (new − old)/old × 100. For "x% of total" questions, work with the given total directly rather than summing the parts. In pie charts, a sector of angle θ represents θ/360 of the total; 1% of the circle is 3.6°. If two years' shares are given as percentages of different totals, convert to absolute values before comparing — comparing percentages across different totals is the standard trap.

    Estimation over precision: when options are wide apart, round ruthlessly (47.3 → 47 or even 50). Compute ratios by cancelling zeros first. For averages over a table row, check whether the question wants a simple average or a weighted one — "average production per year" is simple; "average price across products" is weighted by quantity.

    Common DI question templates: (1) ratio of two quantities in a given year; (2) percentage increase/decrease between years; (3) the year with maximum/minimum growth; (4) how many items exceed a threshold; (5) average over a period. Recognise the template, apply the formula, move on. Never recompute a value you already found for an earlier part of the same set — carry it forward.

    Trigonometric ratios and standard angles

    For a right triangle with angle θ: sin θ = opposite/hypotenuse, cos θ = adjacent/hypotenuse, tan θ = opposite/adjacent. Memorise this table cold:

    • θ = 0°: sin 0, cos 1, tan 0
    • θ = 30°: sin 1/2, cos √3/2, tan 1/√3
    • θ = 45°: sin 1/√2, cos 1/√2, tan 1
    • θ = 60°: sin √3/2, cos 1/2, tan √3
    • θ = 90°: sin 1, cos 0, tan undefined

    Memory trick: write 0, 1, 2, 3, 4; divide each by 4; take square roots → sin 0°, 30°, 45°, 60°, 90° (0, 1/2, 1/√2, √3/2, 1). Cosines are the same sequence reversed.

    Identities and complementary angles

    ; ; . From these: = 1 + sin 2θ-style expansions are rarely needed — what RRB actually tests is substituting identities to simplify an expression to a number. If you see , write 1 immediately.

    Complementary pairs: sin(90° − θ) = cos θ, cos(90° − θ) = sin θ, tan(90° − θ) = cot θ. So sin 60° = cos 30° = √3/2. Products like sin 10°·... are beyond scope, but sums such as sin²20° + sin²70° collapse to 1 via complementary angles — a favourite one-step question.

    Heights and distances

    Angle of elevation and angle of depression are measured from the horizontal; the angle of depression from the top equals the angle of elevation from the bottom (alternate interior angles) — draw this once and it never confuses you again.

    If a tower of height h subtends 45°, the observer's distance from the base equals h. If it subtends 30°, distance = h√3; if 60°, distance = h/√3. For two angles of elevation α and β from points d apart on the same side: h = d·tanα·tanβ/(tanβ − tanα)... in practice, RRB sets α = 30°, β = 60°, giving h = d√3/2 and distances d/2 and 3d/2 — learn the pattern rather than the formula.

    Traps: mixing up angle of elevation with angle of depression (they are complementary in some problem setups, not equal); forgetting that tan 90° is undefined, so a "directly overhead" setup needs a limit argument, not a tan value; and in DI, answering with the percentage share when the question asked for the absolute value.

    Worked example: squeezing a table dry

    Suppose a table gives a company's exports (₹ crore): 2019 → 120, 2020 → 150, 2021 → 135, 2022 → 180, 2023 → 200. RRB will ask four or five questions on one such set — build every reusable number once. First the differences: +30, −15, +45, +20. Percentage growth 2021→2022 = (180 − 135)/135 × 100 = 33.3%. The year of maximum absolute growth is 2022 (+45), but the year of maximum percentage growth is 2020 (30/120 = 25%) versus 2022 (45/135 = 33.3%) — actually 2022 wins both here, yet examiners deliberately construct tables where absolute and percentage maxima differ, so compute both. Average exports = (120 + 150 + 135 + 180 + 200)/5 = 785/5 = 157; years above the average: 2022 and 2023 only. Notice the discipline: one column of differences and one total serve every question. If a question asks "in how many years did exports exceed ₹160 crore", you scan the original row, not your computed numbers — 2022 and 2023, i.e. 2.

    Ratio-type follow-ups: "What is the ratio of 2023 exports to 2019 exports?" — 200:120 = 5:3 after cancelling 40. "If 2024 exports grow at the average annual growth rate of 2019–2023…" — first find the total growth factor 200/120 = 1.667 over 4 years; RRB rarely asks for the CAGR itself, but "simple average of yearly percentage growths" is fair game: (25 − 10 + 33.3 + 11.1)/4 ≈ 14.85%. Watch the sign on negative-growth years; dropping the minus is the classic error.

    Worked example: pie-chart shares

    A ₹720-crore budget is split: Education 25%, Health 20%, Defence 30%, Others 25%. Education's share = 0.25 × 720 = ₹180 crore; Health's sector angle = 0.20 × 360° = 72°. If Defence spending rises 10% next year while the total stays fixed, Defence becomes 216 × 1.1 = ₹237.6 crore — and some other head must shrink, which is exactly how "which head was cut" questions are framed. Now the standard trap: in 2024 the total was ₹600 crore with Education at 30% (₹180 crore); in 2025 the total is ₹720 crore with Education at 25% (₹180 crore). The share fell but the amount is unchanged — any question comparing years must convert percentages to absolute rupees first. A second trap: two pie charts for two years with different totals side by side. Never compare slice sizes visually across pies; convert each slice to its rupee value using its own total.

    Trigonometry beyond sine, cosine, tangent

    RRB expects you to handle the reciprocals fluently: sec θ = 1/cos θ, cosec θ = 1/sin θ, cot θ = 1/tan θ. Their standard-angle values follow directly from the sine/cosine table: sec 60° = 2, cosec 30° = 2, cot 45° = 1, tan 60° = √3 so cot 60° = 1/√3. Two identities do most of the simplification work: 1 − sin²θ = cos²θ and 1 − cos²θ = sin²θ. So √(1 − sin²40°) = cos 40° (taking the positive root for acute angles), and sin²25° + cos²25° + tan²45° = 1 + 1 = 2. Expressions like (sec²θ − 1) collapse to tan²θ in one step; (cosec²θ − cot²θ) is just 1. When an expression mixes angles, look for complementary pairs first: sin²20° + sin²70° = sin²20° + cos²20° = 1, and sec 35°·sin 55° = sec 35°·cos 35° = 1.

    Edge values that get tested: at 0°, sin and tan are 0 while cos and sec are 1; cosec 0° and cot 0° are undefined. At 90° the roles flip: sin and cosec are 1, cos/sec/tan/cot are 0 or undefined. A question like "the value of cos 0° × sec 0°" is 1 × 1 = 1 — trivial once the edges are memorised, and a free mark if they are.

    Heights and distances: master the 30°–60° pair

    The single most-tested setup: from two points on the ground d metres apart, the angles of elevation of a tower's top are 30° and 60°. Let the nearer point be x from the base: h = x·tan60° = x√3 and h = (x + d)·tan30° = (x + d)/√3. Equating gives x√3 = (x + d)/√3, so 3x = x + d, x = d/2 and h = d√3/2. With d = 60 m: the nearer distance is 30 m, the farther 90 m, and h = 30√3 ≈ 51.96 m. Memorise this pattern — nearer = d/2, farther = 3d/2, height = d√3/2 — because RRB reuses it with different numbers every cycle.

    Ladder and shadow problems: a 10-m ladder leaning at 60° to the ground reaches 10·sin60° = 5√3 ≈ 8.66 m up the wall, with its foot 10·cos60° = 5 m from the wall. A pole casting a shadow equal to its height means the sun's elevation is 45° (tan θ = height/shadow = 1). If the shadow is √3 times the height, the elevation is 30°. These reduce to "which standard angle has this tan value" — keep the tan column of your table (0, 1/√3, 1, √3) ready for instant matching.

    DI traps RRB loves

    Five traps account for most lost DI marks. Units: a table in "₹ lakh" beside options in "₹ crore" — convert before computing. "Increase to" vs "increase by": growing to 150% is a 50% increase; growing by 150% lands at 250%. Average of percentages: averaging 10% and 20% growth gives 15% only if the bases are equal — with different bases you must work in absolute values. Share vs absolute: restated once more because it appears every year — a falling percentage share can hide a rising absolute amount when the total grows. Reading the wrong series: in multi-line graphs, trace each line with your finger from the legend to the data point; the two lines always cross exactly where the question is asked.

    4. General Reasoning

    Reasoning is the most scoring section across NTPC, Group D and ALP because every question is self-contained — no formulas to memorise, just clean logic applied fast. The skill is pattern recognition; each type below has a fixed solving ritual.

    Analogies and classification

    Analogy ritual: state the relationship in words first, then apply it. "Doctor : Hospital" — the relation is "works at". Then "Teacher : ?" → School. RRB relations repeat: worker–workplace, tool–user, cause–effect, part–whole, quantity–unit, word–synonym/antonym. For number analogies, test operations in order: square/cube, then ±, then digit sums. Example: 7 : 50 — 7² + 1 = 50, so 9 : 82.

    Classification (odd one out): check the same properties — vowel/consonant counts in words, prime vs composite in numbers, living vs non-living, male/female pairs. If three options share a property and one doesn't, that's your answer; don't overthink second-order patterns unless forced.

    Coding–decoding

    Learn the alphabet positions: A1, E5, J10, O15, T20, Z26 — vowels first, then the round numbers. EJOTY (E5, J10, O15, T20, Y25) is the classic mnemonic. Opposite letters sum to 27: A↔Z, B↔Y, C↔X, M↔N. Most RRB codes are one of: forward/backward shift (check the first and last letters), position-number substitution, reversal, or opposite-letter pairing. For "if CAT = 24" type questions, test A=1 sum (3+1+20=24) before anything else.

    Blood relations

    Draw a quick family tree — never solve in your head. Use + for male, − for female. Decode step by step: "A is the brother of B's mother" → B's mother is A's sister (or A is B's uncle). "Only son of my grandfather" = father or uncle; "only daughter of my mother" = self (if female). Gender words matter: "child" is gender-neutral, "son/daughter" is not. RRB's favourite twist is asking the relation "of X to Y" — direction matters; reverse it and the answer flips.

    Series completion

    Ritual for number series: first check differences, then ratios, then alternating patterns, then squares/cubes, then digit operations. Two interleaved series (odd and even positions separate) are common: 2, 5, 4, 10, 6, 15 — odd positions 2,4,6; even positions 5,10,15. Prime-number series and n²±1, n³±1 patterns appear regularly. For letter series, convert to positions and apply the number-series ritual. Always verify your rule on at least two steps before committing.

    Direction sense and ranking

    Draw the compass: North up, then clockwise N–E–S–W. Left/right turns are relative to facing direction — a person facing North turning left faces West. Shortcut for final direction after turns: right turns = clockwise, count in 90° steps. For distance: break movement into North–South and East–West components separately, then apply Pythagoras for the shortest distance.

    Ranking ritual: total = (position from left) + (position from right) − 1. If A is 7th from the left and 12th from the right, total = 18. For "how many between" questions: between = total − (pos₁ + pos₂). People between A and B = |position difference| − 1. Watch for "from the left end" vs "from the left of B" — the second is relative, not absolute.

    Syllogisms and statement–conclusion

    Draw Venn diagrams. "All A are B" → A sits inside B. "No A is B" → separate circles. "Some A are B" → overlapping circles. A conclusion follows only if it is true in every possible diagram, not just one. The two validity rules that solve most questions: (1) a conclusion with "all" needs an "all" chain — two "some" statements can never yield an "all" conclusion; (2) if any premise is negative ("no"/"some...not"), the conclusion cannot be affirmative.

    Exam temperament: reasoning rewards the first instinct after the ritual — over-verification burns time. Do the 25 easiest reasoning questions first in the paper; puzzles with 4+ conditions go last. Keep a fixed order of attack and never reshuffle mid-exam.

    Seating arrangements: the ritual

    Circular, facing the centre: fix one person at the top and build clockwise. Place every definite statement first ("B sits second to the right of A"), save "either/or" clues for last, and never assume genders or unmentioned relations. Facing the centre, a person's right is your clockwise direction — this single fact resolves half the confusion. Worked miniature: five friends A, B, C, D, E sit around a circle facing the centre. B is second to the right of A; C is immediately to the left of B; D is not next to A. Fix A at the top. Second to A's right (clockwise) is two seats clockwise → B. C is immediately left of B, i.e. one seat anticlockwise from B. That leaves two seats; D cannot be next to A, so D takes the seat two away from A and E fills the last. Verify: is anyone "next to A" violated? Read every clue against the final circle once more before answering.

    Linear rows: left and right are from the sitters' perspective. If they face north, their right is your right as you look at the diagram — but if they face south, left and right flip. Mark the facing direction at the top of your rough work. For "A is third to the left of B" questions, anchor B first, then count. When two clues share a person ("C sits between A and D"), place that person first and hang the others off them. Double-row (facing each other) problems: pair by "sits opposite" and treat each pair as one unit.

    Statement–assumption and assertion–reason

    An assumption is unstated but necessary: the statement cannot be true without it. Test each option by negation — if negating the option destroys the statement, it is a valid assumption. "The government has decided to build a new railway line" assumes funds can be arranged and that the line is needed; it does not assume the line will be profitable, since a decision can rest on connectivity, not profit. Beware options that merely restate the statement (not assumptions) or go far beyond it (too strong to be necessary).

    Assertion–Reason gives two sentences; judge each true/false independently, then check whether the reason explains the assertion. Both can be true while the reason is not the explanation — the classic trap. Example: Assertion — "A freely suspended magnet aligns north–south." Reason — "The Earth itself behaves like a huge magnet." Both true, and the reason does explain the assertion. Contrast: Assertion — "Water boils at 100°C at sea level." Reason — "Atmospheric pressure decreases with altitude." Both true, but the reason does not explain this assertion (it explains why the boiling point changes with altitude). Mark "both true, R not the explanation" without hesitation.

    Non-verbal: figures, mirrors and water images

    Counting figures: for triangles in a triangle subdivided by lines from the apex, count systematically by size — smallest first, then pairs, then the whole. For squares in a grid, use the formula: in an n×n grid there are n² + (n−1)² + … + 1² squares. A 3×3 grid has 9 + 4 + 1 = 14 squares. Missing this formula costs a full minute; with it, the answer is ten seconds. Count rectangles the same way but with n(n+1)/2 per direction: a 3×3 grid has (6 × 6) = 36 rectangles.

    Mirror image = lateral inversion: left and right swap, top and bottom stay. Hold the mirror vertically beside the figure. Water image = vertical inversion: top and bottom swap, left and right stay — as if reflected in water below the figure. Letters and digits have their own behaviour: in a mirror, only A, H, I, M, O, T, U, V, W, X, Y stay unchanged (B, C, D, E all flip laterally); in water, B, C, D, E, H, I, O, X stay the same. When in doubt, sketch the flip on rough paper rather than visualising — one careless flip ruins the question.

    Paper folding and cutting: track folds as mirror operations. A hole punched after two folds unfolds into 2² = 4 holes; after three folds, 8. Draw the final fold's shape, punch the hole, then unfold step by step, mirroring the holes across each fold line. Cubes and dice nets: opposite faces never touch in the net — if two faces share an edge in the net they are adjacent, and any option showing them opposite is impossible. This one rule eliminates most wrong dice options instantly.

    Calendar and clock rapid methods

    Calendar: work in odd days. A non-leap year has 1 odd day, a leap year 2. To find the weekday of a date, count odd days from a known reference. Leap years: divisible by 4, except century years which must be divisible by 400 — so 2000 was a leap year but 1900 was not. The calendar repeats every 400 years. A handy anchor: 1 January 2000 was a Saturday; 1 January 2025 was a Wednesday. Shortcut for "what day was 15 August 1947": compute odd days from 1 Jan 1901 (a Tuesday) — or simply remember that RRB usually gives a same-year reference, so you only count within one year.

    Clocks: angle = |30H − 5.5M| degrees; if it exceeds 180°, take 360° minus it for the smaller angle. The hands move at 5.5° per minute relative speed, so they coincide every 360/5.5 = 65 5/11 minutes (about 65.45), i.e. 11 times in 12 hours — never 12, because the 11th-to-12th coincidence merges at 12:00. At 3:15 the angle is |90 − 82.5| = 7.5°; at 6:00 it is 180°. Hands are at right angles 22 times in 12 hours and opposite 11 times. Memorise these three numbers — 11, 22, 65 5/11 — and a third of clock questions answer themselves.

    Venn diagrams for survey data

    Beyond syllogisms, Venn diagrams solve "survey" questions with the formulas: only-A = A − both; neither = total − (A + B − both). Example: 80 people, 45 like bus, 40 like train, 15 like both. Only bus = 30, only train = 25, at least one = 45 + 40 − 15 = 70, neither = 80 − 70 = 10. For three sets: total = A + B + C − (pairwise intersections) + all three + neither. RRB's favourite twist is giving "only A", "only B" and "both" and asking for "A but not B" — which is just "only A", testing whether you notice. Always label each region of your diagram with its number before reading the question's final ask.

    Data sufficiency and input–output

    Data sufficiency asks whether the statements answer the question — not what the answer is. Check statement I alone, then statement II alone, then both together. "Both needed" is correct only if neither suffices alone. Do not carry information from statement I into your check of statement II. The commonest trap: statement I alone suffices but the question tempts you to combine — the answer is "I alone", not "both".

    Input–output: machines rearrange words/numbers step by step. Track one element per step: numbers usually sort (ascending or descending), words usually alphabetise, and they often alternate. Write step I, II, III and spot the rule by watching a single number's journey. Once the rule is fixed, you can jump to any step without writing the intermediate ones — but verify the rule on two elements, because a wrong rule compounds silently.

    5. Physics

    RRB General Science leans on conceptual physics with numericals in ALP/JE papers. The pattern: one-line facts in NTPC/Group D ("unit of power is..."), formula application in technical posts. Learn facts and formulas as pairs.

    Motion, force and Newton's laws

    ; velocity adds direction; acceleration = change in velocity/time. Equations of motion: v = u + at; s = ut + ½at²; v² = u² + 2as. For free fall, a = g ≈ 9.8 m/s² (use 10 m/s² when the paper's options are round numbers).

    Newton's laws: (1) inertia — a body resists change in its state of rest or motion; (2) F = ma; (3) every action has an equal and opposite reaction. Momentum = mv; its SI unit is kg·m/s. Law of conservation of momentum governs collisions and recoil — a gun recoils because the bullet's forward momentum must be balanced. Friction opposes relative motion; static friction exceeds kinetic friction. Weight = mg; mass is constant everywhere, weight changes with g — on the Moon, weight is 1/6th of Earth's while mass is unchanged.

    Work, energy and power

    Work = force × displacement (in the force's direction); its unit is the joule. Energy is the capacity to do work. Kinetic energy = ½mv²; potential energy = mgh. Power = work/time, unit watt (1 HP ≈ 746 W — a favourite one-liner). Energy conversions: a pendulum's swing is PE ↔ KE; a dynamo converts mechanical energy to electrical; a microphone converts sound to electrical and a loudspeaker reverses it; a solar cell converts light directly to electrical energy. Efficiency is never 100% — some energy always dissipates as heat.

    Gravitation: F = Gm₁m₂/r². Escape velocity from Earth ≈ 11.2 km/s; orbital velocity of a low satellite ≈ 8 km/s. Geostationary satellites orbit at ~35,800 km above the equator with a 24-hour period — this is why TV dish antennas point at fixed spots.

    Electricity and magnetism

    Ohm's law: V = IR. Power in a circuit: P = VI = I²R = V²/R. Series: same current, voltages add, R_total = R₁ + R₂. Parallel: same voltage, currents add, 1/R_total = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂. Household appliances are wired in parallel so each gets full voltage. Fuse wire is made of low-melting-point alloy (lead–tin) and is connected in series. The Earth's magnetic field makes a freely suspended magnet align north–south; the magnetic field inside a solenoid is uniform.

    AC vs DC: household supply is AC (230 V, 50 Hz in India — a guaranteed one-liner). A transformer works only on AC, stepping voltage up or down via mutual induction; it cannot work on DC. Electric motors convert electrical to mechanical energy; generators do the reverse.

    Light, sound and heat

    Light travels at ≈ 3 × 10⁸ m/s in vacuum; sound at ≈ 343 m/s in air (at 20°C) — light is roughly a million times faster, which is why lightning precedes thunder. Reflection: angle of incidence = angle of reflection. Refraction bends light toward the normal when entering a denser medium; total internal reflection needs a denser-to-rarer boundary beyond the critical angle — the principle behind optical fibres and sparkling diamonds.

    Mirrors and lenses: concave mirrors converge (used in headlights, shaving mirrors, solar furnaces); convex mirrors diverge and give a wider field of view (vehicle rear-view mirrors, always forming diminished erect images). Convex lenses converge (magnifying glass, camera); concave lenses diverge (correcting myopia — shortsightedness; convex lenses correct hypermetropia — longsightedness). Dispersion through a prism splits white light into seven colours (VIBGYOR); red deviates least, violet most — rainbows form by dispersion plus internal reflection in droplets.

    Sound needs a medium — it cannot travel in vacuum. Pitch depends on frequency; loudness on amplitude. Ultrasonic = above 20 kHz (used in SONAR, medical imaging); infrasonic = below 20 Hz. Heat transfer: conduction (solids), convection (fluids), radiation (no medium needed — how the Sun's heat reaches us). A black body is the best absorber and the best emitter. Water's anomalous expansion: maximum density at 4°C — why lakes freeze top-down and aquatic life survives winter.

    One-liner bank (high repeat rate): unit of force — newton; unit of pressure — pascal; unit of frequency — hertz; unit of electric charge — coulomb; unit of capacitance — farad; unit of inductance — henry; unit of luminous intensity — candela; unit of magnetic flux — weber. The speed of light in vacuum is the universal speed limit — nothing with mass reaches it.

    6. Chemistry

    RRB chemistry is fact-dense but narrow: atomic structure, the periodic table, acids–bases–salts, metals, and everyday chemical names. Roughly 70% of questions come from these five zones.

    Atoms, elements and the periodic table

    An atom has protons and neutrons in the nucleus and electrons in shells. Atomic number = number of protons (defines the element); mass number = protons + neutrons. Isotopes share the atomic number but differ in neutrons (e.g. C-12, C-14); isobars share the mass number across different elements.

    Electron shells hold 2, 8, 18, 32 (2n²). The periodic table: periods are horizontal rows (7), groups are vertical columns (18). Moving left to right across a period, atomic size decreases and electronegativity increases; moving down a group, size increases and reactivity of metals increases. Metals sit on the left, non-metals on the right, metalloids (B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te) along the staircase boundary.

    Firsts and mosts: lightest element — hydrogen; lightest metal — lithium; most abundant element in the universe — hydrogen; most abundant in Earth's crust — oxygen (~47%); most abundant metal in the crust — aluminium (~8%); most electronegative element — fluorine; only liquid metal at room temperature — mercury; only liquid non-metal — bromine. Diamond and graphite are allotropes of carbon — same element, different structure; diamond is the hardest natural substance, graphite conducts electricity.

    Chemical bonding and reactions

    Ionic bonds transfer electrons (NaCl); covalent bonds share them (H₂, CH₄); metallic bonds pool electrons among metal atoms (explaining conductivity and malleability). Valency is combining capacity — learn the common ones: Na/K/Ag +1; Ca/Mg/Zn/Ba +2; Al +3; O/S −2; Cl/Br/I −1.

    Reaction types: combination (A + B → AB), decomposition (AB → A + B — e.g. CaCO₃ heated gives CaO + CO₂), displacement (Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu), double displacement (AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl + NaNO₃). Oxidation is loss of electrons (or gain of oxygen); reduction is gain of electrons — OIL RIG (Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain). The reactivity series begins K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > H > Cu > Ag > Au — a more reactive metal displaces a less reactive one from its salt solution.

    Acids, bases and salts

    Acids taste sour, turn blue litmus red, and have pH below 7; bases taste bitter, turn red litmus blue, pH above 7. pH 7 is neutral. Strong acids: HCl, H₂SO₄, HNO₃. Strong bases: NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)₂. Antacids (mild bases like Mg(OH)₂) neutralise excess stomach HCl — acidity is literally an acid–base neutralisation in your body.

    Salts to memorise: common salt — NaCl; washing soda — Na₂CO₃·10H₂O; baking soda — NaHCO₃; plaster of Paris — CaSO₄·½H₂O (sets with water to gypsum, CaSO₄·2H₂O); bleaching powder — CaOCl₂; blue vitriol — CuSO₄·5H₂O; green vitriol — FeSO₄·7H₂O; alum — K₂SO₄·Al₂(SO₄)₃·24H₂O. Baking soda + acid releases CO₂ — the chemistry behind both baking and fire extinguishers. Chlorine is used to disinfect drinking water; excess is removed because it is toxic.

    Metals, non-metals and everyday chemistry

    Properties: metals are lustrous, malleable, ductile, and good conductors; non-metals are generally brittle and poor conductors. Iron + carbon = steel; copper + tin = bronze; copper + zinc = brass; aluminium + copper (+ Mg, Mn) = duralumin (aircraft bodies). Stainless steel is iron + chromium (+ nickel) — chromium's oxide layer resists rust. Galvanisation coats iron with zinc to prevent rusting; painting and oiling work the same way by blocking air and moisture.

    Everyday chemistry one-liners: LPG is propane + butane (the smell comes from added ethyl mercaptan); CNG is mainly methane; biogas is methane + CO₂; dry ice is solid CO₂; laughing gas is N₂O; tear gas is chloropicrin; quicklime is CaO and slaked lime is Ca(OH)₂. Photosynthesis: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ (in chlorophyll, using sunlight). Respiration reverses it to release energy. Hard water contains Ca/Mg salts and wastes soap; boiling removes temporary hardness.

    Traps: confusing washing soda (Na₂CO₃) with baking soda (NaHCO₃) — RRB sets them as adjacent options; calling graphite an insulator (it conducts); saying rust is just "iron + water" (it needs oxygen too); and mixing up oxidation/reduction direction — fix OIL RIG permanently before the exam.

    7. Biology — Life Science for RRB

    Biology carries heavy weightage in RRB General Science. Questions cluster around five areas: the human body, nutrition and deficiency diseases, communicable diseases and vaccines, plant physiology, and genetics and ecology.

    The cell — the unit of life

    The cell was first observed by Robert Hooke in 1665 in a slice of cork (he coined the word "cell"). The cell theory was proposed by Schleiden and Schwann (1838–39): all living things are made of cells, and the cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life. Key organelles RRB asks about: the nucleus (control centre, contains DNA), mitochondria (the "powerhouse of the cell" — releases energy through respiration), ribosomes (protein synthesis), chloroplasts (photosynthesis, plants only), and the cell wall (plants only; animal cells have only a cell membrane). The longest cell in the human body is the neuron (nerve cell). Mature red blood cells have no nucleus — a classic trap.

    Human body systems

    Circulatory system: The heart has 4 chambers — two atria (upper) and two ventricles (lower). Blood circulation was discovered by William Harvey (1628); humans have double circulation (blood passes through the heart twice per cycle). A normal resting heartbeat is about 72 beats per minute. The aorta is the largest artery, the inferior vena cava the largest vein, and capillaries the smallest vessels. Blood is ~55% plasma and ~45% cells: RBCs (carry oxygen via haemoglobin, live ~120 days, made in red bone marrow, no nucleus), WBCs (fight infection — immunity), and platelets (blood clotting). Blood groups were discovered by Karl Landsteiner (1901): O-negative is the universal donor, AB-positive is the universal recipient (trap: not O-positive).

    Digestive system: Digestion begins in the mouth (salivary amylase/ptyalin acts on starch), continues in the stomach (hydrochloric acid + pepsin act on proteins), and is completed in the small intestine, where villi absorb nutrients. The liver — the largest gland — produces bile, which emulsifies fats (bile has no enzymes). The pancreas secretes insulin (deficiency causes diabetes) and digestive enzymes. The small intestine is the longest organ of the body; the large intestine mainly absorbs water.

    Respiratory system: Air travels via the nasal passage, trachea and bronchi to the alveoli, where gas exchange happens. Adults breathe 16–20 times per minute at rest; haemoglobin in RBCs carries the oxygen.

    Excretory system: The functional unit of the kidney is the nephron (about a million per kidney). The main nitrogenous waste is urea, removed in urine. The skin (sweat) and lungs (CO₂) are also excretory organs.

    Nervous system: The brain's three key parts are the cerebrum (thought, memory, voluntary action), the cerebellum (balance and coordination), and the medulla oblongata (breathing, heartbeat — involuntary). Sudden responses travel the reflex arc via the spinal cord.

    Skeletal system: An adult has 206 bones. The femur (thigh bone) is the longest and strongest bone; the stapes in the middle ear is the smallest. There are 12 pairs of ribs.

    Endocrine glands (ductless, release hormones into blood): the pituitary is the "master gland"; the thyroid secretes thyroxine (needs iodine — deficiency causes goitre); the pancreas secretes insulin and glucagon; the adrenal glands release adrenaline.

    Plant biology and genetics

    Photosynthesis (6CO₂ + 6H₂O → sunlight/chlorophyll → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂) happens in leaf chloroplasts; stomata handle gas exchange, and transpiration is water loss from leaves. Plant hormones: auxin (growth), gibberellin (stem elongation), cytokinin (cell division), ethylene (fruit ripening). Gregor Mendel is the father of genetics (pea-plant experiments, laws of inheritance). The DNA double helix was described by Watson and Crick in 1953 using Rosalind Franklin's X-ray data. Humans have 46 chromosomes (23 pairs); sex is determined by the X and Y chromosomes — the father's sperm decides the child's sex.

    Ecology in one paragraph

    Producers (green plants) form the first trophic level; Lindeman's 10% law says only ~10% of energy passes to the next level. Decomposers (bacteria, fungi) recycle nutrients. The ozone layer absorbs harmful UV radiation; key greenhouse gases are CO₂, methane and nitrous oxide. Project Tiger began in 1973; India's first biosphere reserve was the Nilgiri (1986).

    Common traps: universal donor is O-negative, not O; the liver is the largest gland but the skin is the largest organ; the small intestine is the longest organ; vitamin D comes mainly from sunlight, not food; insulin comes from the pancreas, not the liver.

    8. History — Ancient, Medieval & Modern India

    RRB history questions are overwhelmingly date-and-person based: who founded what, which battle happened when, and which movement belongs to which year. The strategy is simple — lock the chronology first, then attach one memorable fact to each date. This chapter gives you the complete RRB-relevant spine of Indian history.

    Ancient India

    Indus Valley Civilisation (c. 2600–1900 BCE): Harappa was excavated by Daya Ram Sahni (1921) and Mohenjo-daro by R.D. Banerji (1922). Town planning, the Great Bath (Mohenjo-daro), the dockyard at Lothal (Gujarat), and Dholavira (Gujarat) are the signature facts. Vedic period: the Rigveda is the oldest text; society was organised into janas, and early iron appears in the later Vedic age.

    Buddhism and Jainism (6th century BCE): Gautama Buddha was born in Lumbini (563 BCE), attained enlightenment at Bodh Gaya, gave his first sermon at Sarnath, and attained mahaparinirvana at Kushinagar. Mahavira, the 24th Tirthankara (the first was Rishabhanatha), was born in Kundagrama, Vaishali (599 BCE). Trap: Buddha's birthplace is Lumbini, not Kapilavastu (that was his childhood home).

    Mauryan Empire: Chandragupta Maurya founded the empire in 321 BCE with the guidance of Kautilya (Chanakya), author of the Arthashastra. Ashoka fought the Kalinga War in 261 BCE, after which he embraced Buddhism and spread Dhamma through rock and pillar edicts. Alexander's invasion reached India in 326 BCE (Battle of Hydaspes/Jhelum against King Porus).

    Gupta Age — called the Golden Age of ancient India: Chandragupta II (Vikramaditya), the scholar Aryabhata (zero, pi, earth's rotation), the poet Kalidasa (Shakuntala, Meghaduta), and Nalanda University (founded by Kumaragupta) all belong here. Harshavardhana (606–647 CE) ruled from Kannauj; the Chinese traveller Hiuen Tsang visited his court and wrote the Si-yu-ki. (Fa-Hien visited earlier, during the Gupta period.)

    Medieval India

    Delhi Sultanate: Qutb-ud-din Aibak founded the Slave (Mamluk) dynasty in 1206 and began the Qutub Minar (completed by Iltutmish). Iltutmish made Delhi the capital and introduced the iqta system. Razia Sultana was the first and only woman ruler of the Sultanate. Alauddin Khalji (1296–1316) is remembered for market-control reforms and repelling Mongol invasions. Muhammad bin Tughlaq is remembered for failed experiments — the token currency and shifting the capital to Daulatabad. Firoz Shah Tughlaq dug canals and founded cities.

    Vijayanagara Empire: founded in 1336 by Harihara and Bukka; its greatest ruler was Krishnadevaraya (1509–1529), author of Amuktamalyada. The Bhakti movement ran parallel — Kabir, Guru Nanak (born 1469), Mirabai and Tulsidas (Ramcharitmanas).

    Mughal Empire: Babur won the First Battle of Panipat (1526) using gunpowder and field artillery, founding the empire. Akbar (1556–1605) proclaimed Din-i-Ilahi (1582), abolished the jizya, and had his reign recorded by Abul Fazl in the Akbarnama. Then Jahangir, Shah Jahan (builder of the Taj Mahal, 1632–1653), and Aurangzeb (died 1707 — the empire's decline began after him). Shivaji was crowned Chhatrapati at Raigad in 1674, founding the Maratha state.

    Modern India — British rule and the freedom struggle

    The East India Company received its charter in 1600. British political control began with the Battle of Plassey (1757) — Robert Clive defeated Siraj-ud-Daulah — and was consolidated by the Battle of Buxar (1764). The Revolt of 1857 began on 29 March 1857 at Barrackpore when Mangal Pandey attacked British officers; Bahadur Shah Zafar was declared emperor, Rani Lakshmibai of Jhansi and Tatya Tope led the fighting. After the revolt, the Crown took direct control in 1858 (Queen Victoria's Proclamation).

    Indian National Congress was founded in 1885 by A.O. Hume; its first session was in Bombay under W.C. Bonnerjee. Curzon's Partition of Bengal (1905) triggered the Swadeshi movement; the partition was annulled in 1911, when the capital shifted from Calcutta to Delhi.

    Gandhian movements — learn these as a sequence: Champaran (1917), Kheda and Ahmedabad (1918); Non-Cooperation (1920), suspended after Chauri Chaura (1922); Civil Disobedience (1930), launched with the Dandi March on 12 March 1930 (Salt Law); Quit India (8 August 1942) with the slogan "Do or Die". The Cabinet Mission came in 1946; India became independent on 15 August 1947, with Partition creating Pakistan.

    Revolutionaries: Bhagat Singh threw a bomb in the Central Legislative Assembly (1929) and was hanged on 23 March 1931 with Rajguru and Sukhdev (trap: not 1928). Chandrashekhar Azad died in a shootout at Allahabad (1931); the Kakori train robbery was in 1925. Subhas Chandra Bose founded the Forward Bloc (1939), led the Azad Hind Fauj/INA from 1943, and gave the call "Give me blood, and I shall give you freedom" and "Jai Hind".

    Common traps: the INC was founded in 1885 (not 1886); the Dandi March began on 12 March 1930 but the Civil Disobedience year is simply 1930; Bhagat Singh was hanged in 1931, not 1928; Ashoka's Kalinga war was 261 BCE, and he embraced Buddhism after it; the First Battle of Panipat (1526, Babur) is different from the Second (1556, Akbar vs Hemu) and Third (1761, Ahmad Shah Abdali vs Marathas); Harappa was excavated by Sahni (1921), Mohenjo-daro by Banerji (1922) — the names and years are often swapped in options.

    9. Geography — India and the World

    RRB geography is fact-dense but highly predictable: the same rivers, peaks, states and world records repeat every cycle. Learn the location facts cold, then use the river and mineral tables below as daily revision sheets.

    India — location and extent

    India covers 3.287 million sq km — the 7th largest country in the world, about 2.4% of the world's land area. It lies between 8°4′N and 37°6′N latitude and 68°7′E and 97°25′E longitude. The Tropic of Cancer (23.5°N) passes through 8 states: Gujarat, Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, West Bengal, Tripura and Mizoram. The Standard Meridian is 82°30′E (passing near Mirzapur, Uttar Pradesh); Indian Standard Time is UTC+5:30. India has 28 states and 8 union territories. The longest land border is with Bangladesh (~4,097 km) and the shortest with Afghanistan (~106 km); the total land frontier is about 15,200 km and the coastline about 7,517 km.

    Physical divisions

    The Himalayas are young fold mountains with three parallel ranges — Himadri (Greater), Himachal (Lesser) and Shiwalik (Outer). India's highest peak is K2/Godwin Austen (8,611 m) in the Karakoram (in PoK); the highest peak entirely within undisputed Indian territory is Kangchenjunga (8,598 m) in Sikkim — a favourite trap. The Northern Plains, formed by the Indus–Ganga–Brahmaputra systems, are the most fertile region. The Peninsular Plateau is the oldest landmass of India. The island groups are the Andaman & Nicobar Islands (Bay of Bengal; Barren Island — India's only active volcano) and Lakshadweep (Arabian Sea; coral islands).

    Climate, soils and agriculture

    India has a monsoon climate. The South-West monsoon (June–September) brings most rainfall in two branches — the Arabian Sea branch and the Bay of Bengal branch; the North-East (retreating) monsoon gives rain to Tamil Nadu in winter. Mawsynram (Meghalaya) is the wettest place on earth. Soils: alluvial (northern plains — most fertile), black/regur (Deccan — best for cotton), red, laterite and desert soils. Cropping seasons: Kharif (sown with monsoon — rice, cotton, maize), Rabi (sown in winter — wheat, mustard, gram), Zaid (summer — watermelon, cucumber). The Green Revolution (1966–67) made India self-sufficient in food grains; M.S. Swaminathan is its father in India (Norman Borlaug globally), and wheat was its first success. India is the world's largest milk producer and the second-largest producer of rice and wheat.

    Minerals

    Coal: Jharia (Jharkhand — largest coalfield); Raniganj (West Bengal — oldest). Iron ore: Odisha (Keonjhar), Karnataka (Bellary), Chhattisgarh (Bailadila). Bauxite: Odisha holds the largest reserves. Mica: Jharkhand (Koderma belt) — India is a leading producer. Gold: Kolar (Karnataka). Petroleum: Mumbai High (offshore, largest field); Digboi (Assam — India's oldest refinery). Manganese: Odisha and Maharashtra.

    World geography

    Continents by area: Asia (largest), Africa, North America, South America, Antarctica, Europe, Australia (smallest). Oceans: the Pacific is the largest and deepest (Mariana Trench, ~11,000 m — the deepest point on earth); then Atlantic, Indian, Southern and Arctic. Longest river: the Nile (~6,650 km); the Amazon carries the largest volume of water. Highest peak: Mount Everest (8,848.86 m) on the Nepal–Tibet border. Longest mountain range: the Andes (South America). Largest hot desert: the Sahara; largest cold desert: Antarctica; the Gobi is Asia's largest desert. The equator passes through Ecuador, Colombia, Brazil, Gabon, Congo, Uganda, Kenya, Somalia, Indonesia and others. Neighbourhood facts RRB loves: Nepal's capital Kathmandu, Bhutan–Thimphu, Bangladesh–Dhaka, Sri Lanka–Sri Jayawardenepura Kotte (Colombo is the commercial capital), Pakistan–Islamabad, Myanmar–Naypyidaw, China–Beijing.

    Common traps: Kangchenjunga vs K2 — know which is the highest within undisputed India; the Nile is the longest river but the Amazon has the greatest discharge; the Tropic of Cancer crosses 8 states (count them, don't guess 7); Kharif = monsoon crops (rice), Rabi = winter crops (wheat) — options love to swap these; Chilika is a lagoon (brackish), not a freshwater lake; Narmada flows west through a rift valley, unlike most peninsular rivers.

    10. Polity & Economy

    Polity questions reward memorising a short list of articles, parts and office-holders; economy questions reward understanding a handful of concepts plus a few flagship facts (RBI, Budget, GST, Five-Year Plans). Both sections are high-scoring because the question bank barely changes year to year.

    The Constitution — making and features

    The Constituent Assembly was formed in 1946 under the Cabinet Mission Plan. It first met on 9 December 1946; Dr. Sachchidananda Sinha was the interim president and Dr. Rajendra Prasad was elected permanent president. The Drafting Committee was chaired by Dr. B.R. Ambedkar. The Constitution was adopted on 26 November 1949 and came into force on 26 January 1950 — celebrated as Republic Day. Originally it had 395 articles, 22 parts and 8 schedules (today roughly 448 articles, 25 parts and 12 schedules — counts vary slightly by source). The Preamble begins "We the people of India"; the words Socialist, Secular and Integrity were added by the 42nd Amendment (1976). Borrowed features: Fundamental Rights from the USA, Directive Principles from Ireland, the parliamentary system from Britain, and federalism with a strong centre from Canada.

    Rights, duties and directive principles

    Fundamental Rights (Part III, Articles 12–35) are justiciable — you can go to court to enforce them. The must-know articles: Article 14 (equality before law), Article 15–16 (non-discrimination, equality of opportunity), Article 19 (six freedoms — speech, assembly, association, movement, residence, profession), Article 21 (protection of life and personal liberty), and Article 32 (right to constitutional remedies — called the "heart and soul" of the Constitution by Ambedkar). Directive Principles (Part IV, Articles 36–51) are non-justiciable — guidelines for the state, not enforceable in court (trap: options often swap "justiciable" between the two). Fundamental Duties (Part IVA, Article 51A) were added by the 42nd Amendment; there were 10, and the 86th Amendment (2002) added an 11th — the duty of parents to provide education to children aged 6–14.

    Union government and Parliament

    The President is the head of state, elected by an electoral college (elected MPs and MLAs — not directly by the people), with a 5-year term; removal is by impeachment (Article 61). The Vice-President is the ex-officio Chairman of the Rajya Sabha. The Prime Minister is the real executive head, appointed by the President, and must command a Lok Sabha majority (Articles 74–75). Parliament = President + Lok Sabha + Rajya Sabha (Article 79). The Lok Sabha (House of the People) has a maximum strength of 552 and a 5-year term unless dissolved; the Rajya Sabha (Council of States) has a maximum of 250 members, is a permanent body (never dissolved), with one-third retiring every two years. Money Bills (Article 110) can be introduced only in the Lok Sabha, and the Speaker certifies whether a bill is a money bill. The Supreme Court (Article 124), established on 26 January 1950, is the apex court; its first Chief Justice was Harilal J. Kania. Judicial review — courts testing laws against the Constitution — is its most powerful tool.

    Three emergencies: Article 352 (national emergency — imposed in 1975), Article 356 (President's Rule in a state), Article 360 (financial emergency — never used). The Seventh Schedule divides subjects into the Union, State and Concurrent Lists. The 73rd Amendment (1992, in force 1993) gave constitutional status to Panchayati Raj; the 74th did the same for urban local bodies. The Election Commission (Article 324) conducts elections; the first Chief Election Commissioner was Sukumar Sen.

    Economy — concepts

    GDP is the value of all final goods and services produced within a country's borders in a year (GNP adds net income from abroad). Nominal GDP is at current prices; real GDP adjusts for inflation. Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level — measured in India mainly by the CPI (Consumer Price Index, base year 2012); WPI tracks wholesale prices. The economy has three sectors: primary (agriculture, mining), secondary (manufacturing), tertiary/services (IT, banking, transport) — the services sector contributes the most to India's GDP. Repo rate is the rate at which the RBI lends short-term funds to banks against securities; raising it tightens liquidity and fights inflation. CRR (cash reserve ratio) and SLR (statutory liquidity ratio) are the other liquidity tools.

    Economy — institutions and milestones

    The Reserve Bank of India was established on 1 April 1935 under the RBI Act 1934, nationalised in 1949, and is headquartered in Mumbai; its first Indian Governor was C.D. Deshmukh. Banks were nationalised in two waves — 1969 (14 banks) and 1980 (6 banks); SBI is the largest public-sector bank. The First Five-Year Plan (1951–56) focused on agriculture (Harrod–Domar model); the Twelfth Plan (2012–17) was the last. The Planning Commission was replaced by NITI Aayog on 1 January 2015 (chairperson: the Prime Minister). The 1991 LPG reforms (Liberalisation, Privatisation, Globalisation) were launched under PM Narasimha Rao with Finance Minister Manmohan Singh. GST — "One Nation, One Tax" — was launched on 1 July 2017 via the 101st Constitutional Amendment. The Union Budget is presented on 1 February (the Constitution calls it the Annual Financial Statement, Article 112). Flagship schemes to know: Jan Dhan Yojana (2014) — financial inclusion; UPI by NPCI (2016) — digital payments; MGNREGA (2006) — rural employment guarantee.

    Common traps: Fundamental Rights are justiciable, Directive Principles are not — never swap them; Article 32 (constitutional remedies), not Article 226 (that's High Courts); the Rajya Sabha is never dissolved; money bills originate only in the Lok Sabha; the 42nd Amendment added both the Preamble words and the Fundamental Duties; GST launched 1 July 2017 (not 2016, when the amendment passed); bank nationalisation was 1969 + 1980; NITI Aayog replaced the Planning Commission in 2015, not 2014.

    11. Railway GK — History, Zones, Production Units & Budget

    This is the highest-yield chapter on the entire site: RRB is the railway's own exam, and railway-awareness questions are effectively free marks. Every fact below has appeared in NTPC, Group D or ALP papers.

    Birth of the railways

    The first train in India ran on 16 April 1853 from Bori Bunder (Mumbai) to Thane — a distance of 34 km — hauled by three locomotives named Sahib, Sindh and Sultan. The Governor-General at the time was Lord Dalhousie, often called the father of Indian Railways. The first electric train ran on 3 February 1925 between Bombay VT and Kurla. Today the network covers roughly 68,000 route km (approximate — among the largest in the world, and the largest managed by a single organisation), with three gauges: broad gauge (1,676 mm), metre gauge (1,000 mm) and narrow gauge (762/610 mm). Project Unigauge converts metre and narrow lines to broad gauge.

    The Railway Budget merger

    For 92 years the Railways had a separate budget, a practice dating to the Acworth Committee (1921). On the recommendation of the Bibek Debroy Committee, the Railway Budget was merged with the General Budget from 2017–18. The last standalone Railway Budget was presented in February 2016 by Suresh Prabhu. Trap: the merger was announced/decided in 2016 but took effect from the 2017–18 budget year.

    Firsts and records

    • First Rajdhani: 1969 (Howrah–New Delhi). First Shatabdi: 1988 (New Delhi–Jhansi). First Vande Bharat: 2019 (New Delhi–Varanasi).
    • First metro: Kolkata, 1984 (Esplanade–Bhowanipore) — India's first underground railway.
    • Longest platform in the world: SSS Hubballi Junction, Karnataka (1,505 m), commissioned 2023 — it overtook Gorakhpur.
    • Longest train route: Vivek Express, Dibrugarh–Kanyakumari (~4,189 km).
    • Fastest train: Vande Bharat (tested at 180 km/h; runs at up to 160 km/h).
    • Highest railway bridge in the world: the Chenab Bridge (359 m above the riverbed) in J&K, on the Udhampur–Srinagar–Baramulla line.
    • Oldest working steam locomotive: the Fairy Queen (1855).
    • UNESCO World Heritage mountain railways: Darjeeling Himalayan Railway (1999), Nilgiri Mountain Railway (2005), Kalka–Shimla Railway (2008).
    • Konkan Railway (Roha–Mangalore, ~760 km) was completed in 1998 — an engineering marvel with 90+ tunnels and 2,000+ bridges.

    Electrification is nearly complete on the broad-gauge network (roughly 97%+ by the mid-2020s; the Railways targets 100% broad-gauge electrification and net-zero carbon by 2030 — treat exact percentages as approximate). The Railways is called the "lifeline of the nation", and Railway Week is observed 10–16 April (marking the first run of 16 April 1853). As of 2026 the Railway Minister is Ashwini Vaishnaw (in office since July 2021) — verify against the latest appointment list before your exam, since ministers can change.

    Common traps: the first train ran in 1853 (not 1854); the three engines were Sahib, Sindh and Sultan; the first electric train was 1925, not 1853; the Railway Budget merged from 2017–18 (announced 2016); Hubballi — not Gorakhpur — now holds the longest-platform record; South Coast Railway (Visakhapatnam) is the 18th zone; ICF (Chennai) is coaches, CLW (Chittaranjan) is locomotives — options swap these.
